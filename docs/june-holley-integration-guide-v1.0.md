## 1. Purpose and Source

The Mission Brief names June Holley's Network Weaving as a foundational practice (Section 2.3) but does not translate that practice into specific platform mechanics, Cell Steward training content, or feature behaviour. This document closes that gap.

**Primary sources, consulted directly for this guide:**
- *Network Weaver Handbook: A Guide to Transformational Networks*, June Holley (2012) — full table of contents reviewed; structure referenced throughout
- *Building Smart Communities through Network Weaving*, Valdis Krebs and June Holley (2002–2006) — the four-phase network topology model (Section 4a below), sourced directly from the original paper
- The Network Weaving Institute (nwinstitute.org) and Network Weaver (networkweaver.com) — June Holley's active organisation, in continuous operation for over 40 years of network practice
- Network Weaver's free resource library (networkweaver.com/resources) — Creative Commons-licensed tools curated by Holley's organisation, several authored by close collaborators and partner organisations, attributed individually below
- Direct quotation is avoided throughout this document per copyright practice; all concepts are paraphrased and cited to their source

**A note on attribution:** Network Weaver's resource library includes June Holley's own work alongside curated contributions from partner practitioners and organisations (Curtis Ogden of the Interaction Institute for Social Change, Change Elemental, Social Change Agency, and others). Where a specific resource is by a collaborator rather than Holley directly, this guide names the actual author. The methodology as a whole — and its application to ResilientSA — is grounded in Holley's Network Weaving framework specifically.

## 3. Gifts Mapping — Grounded in Network Weaving Practice

The Mission Brief's Gifts Profile (Section 2.2) draws on Holley's gifts-based approach to community building, closely related to Asset-Based Community Development. This section makes the practice concrete for the platform's onboarding flow (Mission Brief 9.4, Stage 7).

### 3.1 The Three Questions, Properly Sourced

The Gifts Profile's three founding questions — what do you love to do, what are you naturally good at, what do you care about deeply — follow Holley's practice of starting from abundance rather than deficit (Mission Brief 2.4). This is consistent with the broader Network Weaving Institute's stated commitment to building community power through recognising existing capacity rather than cataloguing need (Section 6 below, Essential Capacities).

### 3.2 Why Gifts Mapping Is a Network Activity, Not a Data Entry Task

A critical distinction the Handbook makes (Part 2, "Connecting to Increase the Effectiveness of Your Network") is that a gifts map only has value once it informs actual introductions — mapping without connecting is incomplete network weaving. This directly shapes how the platform must behave:

**Platform requirement:** A completed Gifts Profile must trigger the intelligence layer's contextual matching (Mission Brief 4.5) within the same onboarding session where possible — not simply store the data for later. The first experience of submitting a Gifts Profile should, wherever the cell has enough existing members, surface at least one potential connection to the Cell Steward immediately. This is the platform's first demonstration of "the magic" (Mission Brief 4.5) and should not be delayed.

## 4. Cell Steward Training — A Concrete Curriculum Outline

The Mission Brief states Cell Stewards are "trained through a dedicated network weaving Grounder programme based on June Holley's methodology" (Section 3.2) but does not define the curriculum. This section provides that outline, structured around the Handbook's Part 1 and Part 5.

### 4.1 Module 1 — Network Leadership Is Not Organisational Leadership

Drawn from Handbook Chapter 2 (Network Leadership). The core distinction Cell Stewards must internalise: a network leader's job is to see the whole network, make introductions, and then step back — not to control, direct, or be the hub through which everything flows. This directly grounds the Mission Brief's description of Cell Stewards as people whose "primary function is connecting people, not administering a system" (Section 3.2).

**Training activity:** Cell Stewards map their own existing relationships in their cell before the platform goes live — a simple paper or whiteboard exercise, not a digital tool — to practise the skill of seeing a network before being asked to read one through platform data.

### 4.2 Module 2 — Becoming a Network Catalyst

Drawn from Handbook Part 1, Chapter 1 ("Becoming a Network Catalyst" — work on yourself first; network catalysts don't work alone; finding other catalysts; being intentional about your network). This module addresses something the Mission Brief's Steward Companion mechanic (Section 3.2) gestures at without fully explaining: Holley's framework insists a network weaver cannot do this work alone, and actively training Stewards to identify their own complementary catalysts is part of the role, not an afterthought.

**Training activity:** Each Cell Steward identifies their Steward Companion as part of training, not afterward — framed explicitly as "finding your fellow catalyst," per the Handbook's own language, rather than a separate administrative appointment.

### 4.3 Module 3 — Trust in Networks

Drawn from Handbook Part 2, Chapter 8 (Trust in Networks). This grounds the platform's Community Value Charter process (Mission Brief 5.4) and Fairness Tribunal (5.5) — both of which depend on trust existing before formal mechanisms are needed, not trust being manufactured by the mechanisms themselves.

**Training activity:** Before drafting the Value Charter, Cell Stewards facilitate a trust-mapping conversation in their cell — who do members already trust, and why — surfacing the cell's existing trust network rather than assuming the Charter creates trust from nothing.

### 4.4 Module 4 — Connecting to Increase Network Effectiveness

Drawn from Handbook Chapter 5. This is the practical skill of facilitating introductions well — not just noticing a match exists, but introducing it in a way that lands. This grounds the Mission Brief's repeated insistence that the intelligence layer surfaces matches but a human delivers them (Section 4.5).

**Training activity:** Role-play practice introducing two members with complementary gifts, with feedback on warmth, specificity, and context — the difference between "you two should meet" and the kind of introduction the Mission Brief describes: *"I thought you should meet Nomsa — she's been looking for exactly this kind of connection"* (Section 4.5).

## 4a. Network Shapes — The Four-Phase Model and What It Means for ResilientSA

This is foundational material missing from the original draft of this guide and deserves its own section, not a footnote. June Holley and Valdis Krebs co-authored a specific, well-documented model — *Building Smart Communities through Network Weaving* (2002–2006) — describing four distinct network topologies a healthy community network moves through. This model has direct, concrete implications for how ResilientSA's onboarding sequence and intelligence layer should behave differently depending on a node's maturity.

### 4a.1 The Four Phases, As Holley and Krebs Define Them

**Phase 1 — Scattered Fragments.** Small, isolated clusters of 1–5 people connected out of necessity, with little or no connection between clusters. Left alone, these fragments stay weak and under-producing.

**Phase 2 — Single Hub-and-Spoke.** A network weaver becomes the central hub, connecting to each scattered cluster individually. Holley and Krebs are explicit and emphatic that this is a *necessary but temporary* phase — it concentrates both power and vulnerability in one node. If the hub fails or leaves, the network collapses back to fragments. The weaver's job during this phase is to learn each cluster's skills, goals, and needs — and then to actively begin connecting clusters to each other, deliberately working to make their own centrality less necessary over time.

**Phase 3 — Multi-Hub Small-World Network.** As the original weaver succeeds, new weavers emerge from within the formerly separate clusters. Multiple hubs now exist, connected to each other by weak ties (per Granovetter, cited directly in the source paper) that may or may not strengthen into permanent connections. This eliminates the single point of failure inherent in Phase 2, and is explicitly named as a more resilient structure.

**Phase 4 — Core/Periphery.** The mature end state. A dense, strongly-connected core of established network weavers and key members, surrounded by a porous periphery of three groups: people new to the community working toward the core, bridges to other communities, and unique outside resources. The periphery brings in new ideas; the core has the strength to act on them. This is described as the structure capable of forming alliances with other well-developed networks in other regions.

### 4a.2 Why This Matters More Than a Single "Network Health" Score

The Mission Brief's Network Health Metrics (Section 6.1) — Connection Density, Hub Identification, Isolate Detection, Bridge Connections, Self-Organisation Index — are currently treated as universal targets, implicitly assuming "more density, more connections, fewer isolates" is always better, in every node, at every point in time. **Holley and Krebs's model shows this is not quite right.** A node in Phase 2 (Single Hub-and-Spoke) is *correctly* hub-dependent — that's not a problem to be immediately corrected, it's a necessary and expected stage. The platform should not flag a new Cell Steward as a "burnout risk hub" in the same way it would flag a hub persisting in a mature, Phase 4 node. The intelligence layer needs to know which phase a node is roughly in before it can correctly interpret what its own metrics mean.

### 4a.3 Concrete Platform Implications

**The `Node.health_state` field (Technical Architecture Document, Section 3.1) should be extended, or a parallel field added, to track network maturity phase** — not as a replacement for the Generative/Stressed/Fragile/Collapsed health states (these measure something different: trust and safety, not topology), but as a second, distinct dimension. A node can be both "Generative" (healthy, safe, trusting) and still correctly in "Phase 2 — Hub and Spoke" because it's simply new. These are not the same axis and should not be conflated.

**Hub Identification (Mission Brief 6.1) needs phase-aware interpretation.** In a Phase 2 node, the Cell Steward being the dominant hub is expected and correct — the Reciprocity Prompt and Steward Companion mechanics (Section 5 of this guide) still apply for the Steward's own wellbeing, but the platform should not present this hub status to the Steward as if it's an anomaly requiring urgent correction. In a Phase 4 node, by contrast, a single dominant hub persisting is a genuine warning sign worth flagging more seriously — the network has failed to mature past dependency on one person.

**The Onboarding Journey (Mission Brief 9.4) is, in Holley and Krebs's terms, the platform's design for guiding a node from Phase 1 to Phase 2.** Stage 6 (Steward Election) creates the hub. Stages 7–9 (Gifts Mapping, Value Charter, First Trades) are the weaver's work of "learning what each cluster knows and needs" before connecting them. This reframes those stages usefully: they are not just bureaucratic steps, they are the documented mechanism by which Phase 1 becomes Phase 2.

**A Cell Steward's explicit, trained goal should be to work toward their own reduced centrality** — moving the cell from Phase 2 toward Phase 3. This deserves to be stated directly in Cell Steward training (Section 4 of this guide) as a named goal, not left implicit: *"Your job is not to become the hub forever. Your job is to notice when two members no longer need you to introduce them, and to actively encourage other members to start doing some of your connecting work."* This is a direct, sourced answer to what "the platform should be forgotten" (Mission Brief 2.3) actually means in structural terms for a single cell, not just the platform overall.

**Inter-cell and inter-node connections (the Trade Exchange's multi-party swaps, and eventually Phase 2's inter-community trade) are, in this model, the weak ties that bridge separate clusters into a Phase 3 or Phase 4 structure.** Holley and Krebs are specific that bridging ties form between groups that are both similar and different — "connect on your similarity, and profit from your diversity." This offers a concrete design principle for how the intelligence layer should weight multi-party swap suggestions across cells or nodes: not purely by need-match efficiency, but with some explicit weight toward connections that bridge otherwise-separate clusters, since these bridging ties are structurally valuable in a way a same-cluster match is not.

**The Regional Resilience Cooperative (Mission Brief 8.5), when it is built in Phase 2, is the platform's eventual Core/Periphery structure at regional scale** — multiple mature Community Resilience Cooperatives (cores) connected to each other, with new and prospective communities, bridge relationships to other regions, and external resource partners (Grounders, government bodies) forming the periphery. This gives the still-unspecified Phase 2 federation architecture (flagged as an open item in the Technical Architecture Document, Section 8) a sourced structural target to design toward, rather than starting from nothing.

### 4a.4 The Ricchiuto Pyramid — Calibrating What "Facilitating an Introduction" Actually Means

The source paper also references a seven-level pyramid of network weaving involvement, developed by Jack Ricchiuto after working with Krebs and Holley, ranging from Level 1 ("suggesting to A that A should talk to B") up to Level 7 ("introducing A to B in person and offering a collaboration opportunity to get them off to a successful partnership"). The paper notes that the most effective community-building work happens at the top two levels, not the bottom ones.

This directly calibrates a detail left vague in the original draft of this guide (Section 4.4, the introduction role-play training): **a Cell Steward's introductions should be trained and evaluated against this scale.** A platform notification merely suggesting "you two should meet" is closer to Level 1–2. The Mission Brief's own example — *"I thought you should meet Nomsa, she's been looking for exactly this kind of connection"* — sits around Level 5. Cell Steward training should explicitly aim Stewards toward Level 6–7 wherever the relationship matters: not just naming the connection, but following up with both parties afterward to nurture it. This is a concrete, sourced training standard, not just a stylistic preference.

## 5. Sustaining Stewards — Network Weaving as "Light Work"

This section directly addresses a gap the bridge identified months ago and never fully closed: protection for the protectors (Mission Brief 6.2). June Holley's network (via collaborator Curtis Ogden, Interaction Institute for Social Change, syndicated on Network Weaver) has written directly and recently about this exact problem — sustaining network weavers without burning them out, particularly in difficult times.

### 5.1 The Source Concept

Ogden's framing names network weaving as "light work" — gentle, steadfast practices of connection, contrasted with the louder, harder-edged work crisis response often demands. The practices named fall into four groups directly relevant to ResilientSA's Steward Companion and Reciprocity Prompt mechanics (Mission Brief 6.2):

**Collective action and mutual care** — including explicitly "protecting our leaders (including protecting them from themselves)" and "keeping in mind excess resources/capacity and offering to others." This is, almost verbatim, the function the Mission Brief assigns to the Steward Companion and the Reciprocity Prompt.

**Practices of wellbeing and connection** — holding space with loving intention, reflecting back people's strengths, care-full listening, respecting silence.

**Inner work and growth** — grounding in humility, not taking oneself too seriously, setting loving boundaries to avoid being overwhelmed, staying curious.

**Spiritual and cultural wisdom** — extending the teachings of elders to present circumstances, honouring connection to land and community, living as though a better world is possible.

### 5.2 Platform and Training Implications

**The Steward Companion role (Mission Brief 3.2, 6.2) should be explicitly trained, not just assigned.** Per Section 5.1's first category — "protecting our leaders, including protecting them from themselves" — the Steward Companion's training should include direct instruction that part of their role is noticing when a Cell Steward is taking on too much, even when the Steward themselves insists they're fine. This is a concrete, sourced answer to the open question from earlier bridge sessions about what "watching for Steward depletion" (Mission Brief 3.2) actually means in practice.

**The Reciprocity Prompt (Mission Brief 6.2) is "light work" made into a feature.** Holley's collaborator network names "keeping in mind excess resources/capacity and offering to others" as a sustaining practice. This is precisely what the Reciprocity Prompt automates at the relationship level — the intelligence layer notices when reciprocity has lapsed and nudges the Steward toward the same practice Network Weaver's own community names as essential to sustaining itself.

**Setting loving boundaries belongs in Cell Steward training (Module 2, Section 4.2 above).** Holley's network explicitly names "setting loving boundaries to keep from being overwhelmed" as inner work required of network weavers. This should be a stated, explicit permission given to Cell Stewards during training — not an implicit expectation they discover only after burning out.

## 6. Essential Capacities — Grounding the Crisis Roles Framework

A separate, directly relevant resource curated by Network Weaver: *Essential Capacities for Equitable Communities* (authored by Change Elemental, curated and shared via Network Weaver's library). This analyses the organisational capacities uniquely required to build community power, and names three core capacities reinforced by several supporting ones.

### 6.1 The Three Core Capacities

**The capacity to organise communities, deepen equity, and cultivate leadership and leaders.** These three, per Change Elemental's analysis, are mutually reinforcing rather than separable.

### 6.2 The Reinforcing Capacities

Capacity to nurture and sustain networks; generate financial resources; change culture and narratives; carry out research, advocacy, and policy implementation; and **provide services and mutual aid.**

### 6.3 Direct Application to the Crisis Roles Framework

This framework offers a sourced structure for the still-unwritten Crisis Roles Framework spec (flagged as open in Mission Brief Section 14 and MISSION_STATUS.md). Mission Brief Section 7.2 already maps gift categories to crisis roles (Infrastructure Responders, Wellbeing Holders, Sense Makers, etc.) — Change Elemental's capacity framework, as curated through Holley's resource library, suggests these crisis roles should be explicitly understood as expressions of the same core capacities a community needs in non-crisis times, not a separate skill set invented for emergencies. **This becomes a founding principle for the Crisis Roles Framework spec when it is written:** crisis roles are not new capacities added on top of normal community life — they are the community's everyday capacity to organise, build equity, and lead, expressed under pressure.
